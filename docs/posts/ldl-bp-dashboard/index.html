Introduction

High LDL cholesterol and high blood pressure are two of the most important modifiable risk factors for heart attack and stroke, and they often show up in the same people. I wanted to know whether, in a large sample of U.S. adults, people with higher LDL also tend to have higher blood pressure, or whether the two risk factors travel more independently than we might assume.

I focused on adults aged 45 to 79 for two reasons. Both LDL and blood pressure typically climb through midlife, and this is the age range where women’s cardiovascular risk profile shifts around menopause, which makes a comparison of men and women especially interesting. I also compared racial and ethnic groups, since the burden of hypertension in the United States is not evenly shared.

The Data

I used the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2017-March 2020 pre-pandemic files, pulled into R with the nhanesA package on September 28, 2026. Three tables were merged on the participant ID (SEQN):

  • P_DEMO for age, sex, and race/ethnicity
  • P_TRIGLY for LDL cholesterol (Friedewald equation, mg/dL)
  • P_BPXO for oscillometric blood pressure, averaged over two or three readings

LDL is measured only in the morning fasting subsample, which is the main reason the final sample is smaller than the full survey:

Step Participants
All participants in P_DEMO 15,560
Interviewed and examined, age 45-79 4,694
…with an LDL result (fasting subsample) 2,180
…and complete data on every variable 1,987

Participants aged 80 were excluded because NHANES codes everyone aged 80 and older as “80”. One participant had an LDL of 7 mg/dL; I kept this value, since very low LDL is plausible with intensive lipid-lowering therapy. Survey weights were not used, so the results describe this sample rather than the U.S. population as a whole.

# =============================================================================
# prep_nhanes.R
# Ingest, merge, and clean NHANES 2017-March 2020 pre-pandemic data
#   P_DEMO   Demographics
#   P_TRIGLY LDL cholesterol (lab; fasting subsample only)
#   P_BPXO   Oscillometric blood pressure (exam)
# =============================================================================

library(here)
library(nhanesA)
library(janitor)
library(tidyverse)   # tidyverse loaded last, per course convention

raw_dir <- here("data", "raw")
dir.create(raw_dir, recursive = TRUE, showWarnings = FALSE)

get_nhanes <- function(table) {
  path <- file.path(raw_dir, paste0(table, ".rds"))
  if (!file.exists(path)) {
    nhanes(table, translated = FALSE) |>
      as_tibble() |>
      saveRDS(path)
  }
  readRDS(path)
}

demo_raw <- get_nhanes("P_DEMO")
trig_raw <- get_nhanes("P_TRIGLY")
bpxo_raw <- get_nhanes("P_BPXO")

dim(demo_raw); dim(trig_raw); dim(bpxo_raw)
names(trig_raw)
names(bpxo_raw)

demo <- demo_raw |>
  select(SEQN, RIDSTATR, RIAGENDR, RIDAGEYR, RIDRETH3)

trig <- trig_raw |>
  select(SEQN, LBDLDL)                      # LDL-C, Friedewald, mg/dL

bpxo <- bpxo_raw |>
  select(SEQN,
         BPXOSY1, BPXOSY2, BPXOSY3,         # systolic readings 1-3
         BPXODI1, BPXODI2, BPXODI3)         # diastolic readings 1-3

merged <- demo |>
  left_join(trig, by = "SEQN") |>
  left_join(bpxo, by = "SEQN") |>
  clean_names()

nrow(merged) == nrow(demo_raw)       # should be TRUE
n_distinct(merged$seqn) == nrow(merged)  # TRUE = no duplicate people

sbp_vars <- c("bpxosy1", "bpxosy2", "bpxosy3")
dbp_vars <- c("bpxodi1", "bpxodi2", "bpxodi3")

eligible <- merged |>
  filter(ridstatr == 2, between(ridageyr, 45, 79)) |>
  mutate(
    n_bp = rowSums(!is.na(pick(all_of(sbp_vars)))),
    sbp  = if_else(n_bp >= 2,
                   rowMeans(pick(all_of(sbp_vars)), na.rm = TRUE), NA_real_),
    dbp  = if_else(n_bp >= 2,
                   rowMeans(pick(all_of(dbp_vars)), na.rm = TRUE), NA_real_),
    sbp  = round(sbp, 1),
    dbp  = round(dbp, 1),
    
    age  = ridageyr,
    ldl  = lbdldl,
    
    sex = factor(riagendr, levels = c(1, 2), labels = c("Male", "Female")),
    
    race_eth = case_when(
      ridreth3 %in% c(1, 2) ~ "Hispanic",
      ridreth3 == 3         ~ "NH White",
      ridreth3 == 4         ~ "NH Black",
      ridreth3 == 6         ~ "NH Asian",
      ridreth3 == 7         ~ "Other/Multiracial"
    ),
    race_eth = factor(race_eth, levels = c("NH White", "NH Black", "Hispanic",
                                           "NH Asian", "Other/Multiracial"))
  )

count(eligible, sex)
count(eligible, race_eth)
summary(eligible$sbp)

nh <- eligible |>
  filter(!is.na(ldl), !is.na(sbp), !is.na(dbp),
         !is.na(sex), !is.na(race_eth), !is.na(age)) |>
  mutate(
    ldl_high = factor(if_else(ldl >= 130, "Yes", "No"), levels = c("Yes", "No")),
    
    ldl_cat = cut(ldl,
                  breaks = c(-Inf, 100, 130, 160, Inf), right = FALSE,
                  labels = c("<100", "100-129", "130-159", "160+")),
    
    bp_cat = case_when(
      sbp >= 140 | dbp >= 90 ~ "Stage 2",
      sbp >= 130 | dbp >= 80 ~ "Stage 1",
      TRUE                   ~ "Normal/Elevated"
    ),
    bp_cat = factor(bp_cat, levels = c("Normal/Elevated", "Stage 1", "Stage 2")),
    
    htn_range = factor(if_else(bp_cat == "Normal/Elevated", "No", "Yes"),
                       levels = c("Yes", "No")),
    
    ldl10 = ldl / 10
  ) |>
  select(seqn, age, sex, race_eth, ldl, ldl10, ldl_high, ldl_cat,
         sbp, dbp, bp_cat, htn_range)

nrow(nh)                        # must be between 500 and 7,500
count(nh, ldl_high, htn_range)  # every cell >= 30 (Analysis D)
count(nh, ldl_cat)              # every group >= 30 (Analysis C)
table(nh$race_eth, nh$bp_cat)   # every cell >= 15 (Analysis E)

stopifnot(
  "Duplicate SEQN values"         = !anyDuplicated(nh$seqn),
  "Sample outside 500-7,500 rule" = between(nrow(nh), 500, 7500),
  "LDL has < 15 unique values"    = n_distinct(nh$ldl) >= 15,
  "SBP has < 15 unique values"    = n_distinct(nh$sbp) >= 15,
  "Implausible LDL value"         = all(nh$ldl > 0 & nh$ldl < 500),
  "Implausible SBP value"         = all(between(nh$sbp, 60, 260)),
  "Implausible DBP value"         = all(between(nh$dbp, 20, 160))
)

Hypotheses

Before looking at the results, I wrote down what I expected:

  1. Adults with LDL of 130 mg/dL or higher have higher mean systolic blood pressure than adults with lower LDL.
  2. Mean systolic blood pressure rises steadily across LDL categories (below 100, 100-129, 130-159, and 160 or higher).
  3. Blood pressure of 130/80 or higher is more common among adults with LDL of 130 or higher.
  4. The mix of blood pressure categories differs across racial and ethnic groups.
  5. The LDL-blood pressure relationship is stronger in women than in men, and differs across racial and ethnic groups.

Results

All intervals are 90% confidence intervals.

Higher LDL did not mean higher average systolic blood pressure, at first glance. Comparing adults with LDL of 130 or higher to everyone else, the difference in mean systolic blood pressure was 0.9 (90% CI: -0.7 to 2.5) mm Hg (Welch’s t test). The interval includes zero, and pooled t, bootstrap, and Wilcoxon rank sum estimates all told the same story, so this result does not hinge on a normality assumption. Across four LDL categories, a one-way ANOVA gave F = 1.82 (p = 0.142), with 0 of 6 Tukey comparisons excluding zero, but LDL category explained only 0.3% of the variation in systolic blood pressure, so even where some groups differ, LDL category on its own tells us very little about a person’s blood pressure. Hypothesis 1 was not supported by this unadjusted comparison.

Hypertensive-range blood pressure was modestly more common with high LDL. Blood pressure of 130/80 or higher was found in 54.6% of adults with LDL of 130 or higher and 49.8% of those with lower LDL, a relative risk of 1.10 (90% CI: 1.02 to 1.18). This supports hypothesis 3. Why would this analysis find an association when the comparison of means did not? The 130/80 threshold also counts diastolic pressure, and it asks about crossing a cutoff rather than about the average, so a shift near the cutoff can show up here without moving the mean much.

Blood pressure categories differed across racial and ethnic groups. The chi-square test of independence gave 64.3 on 8 df (p < 0.001), supporting hypothesis 4. Stage 2 blood pressure ranged from 20.5% of NH White adults to 39.1% of NH Black adults.

Age was hiding the LDL-blood pressure association. Older participants tended to have lower LDL (r = -0.17) but higher systolic blood pressure (r = 0.22). Because age pushes the two in opposite directions, it masks their relationship when age is ignored. After adjusting for age, sex, and race/ethnicity, each 10 mg/dL higher LDL went with a 0.36 (90% CI: 0.18 to 0.54) mm Hg higher mean systolic blood pressure. That is small per unit, but across a 100 mg/dL range of LDL it adds up to about 3.6 mm Hg.

The relationship was steeper in men, the opposite of what I expected. The age-adjusted slope was 0.46 (90% CI: 0.21 to 0.71) mm Hg per 10 mg/dL in men and 0.21 (90% CI: -0.05 to 0.47) in women. The test that directly compares the two slopes gave p = 0.064, which is modest evidence of a difference at my 90% standard. There was no evidence that the slope differed across racial and ethnic groups (p = 0.530). So hypothesis 5 was not supported: the sex difference ran in the other direction, and the ethnic differences did not appear.

Conclusions

In adults aged 45 to 79, higher LDL goes with slightly higher blood pressure, but the association only becomes clear once age is taken into account, and it appears stronger in men than in women. The effect is modest for any one person, yet at the population level a few mm Hg of systolic pressure is meaningful. I can only guess at why the sex difference runs the way it does: statin use may differ between men and women, and changes around menopause may shift LDL and blood pressure somewhat independently. Both are ideas to test, not conclusions.

Limitations

  • Cross-sectional data. Each person was measured once, so these are associations, not effects of LDL on blood pressure.
  • Medication use. Many adults in this age range take statins or blood pressure drugs. If people with high blood pressure are more likely to be on a statin, treatment could weaken or even reverse the LDL-blood pressure association seen here. The NHANES blood pressure and cholesterol questionnaire (P_BPQ) would allow adjusting for this.
  • Fasting subsample, no weights. LDL comes from a morning subsample, and I did not apply survey weights, so the results are not nationally representative.
  • Smaller groups. Estimates for NH Asian and Other/Multiracial adults rest on fewer participants and have wider intervals.

Future Analyses

The natural next step is to add medication use from P_BPQ and refit the models, and to look at diastolic blood pressure alongside systolic. I would also like to try the Martin-Hopkins LDL estimate, which NHANES also provides and which is more accurate than the Friedewald equation when triglycerides are high.

About the Dashboard

The dashboard is a Quarto file using format: dashboard, published with the rest of this site on GitHub Pages. Data cleaning, statistics, and plotting each live in their own R script, and this post reads from the same analysis script, so the numbers always match. The Overview page leads with five headline numbers and an interactive scatter plot. Each analysis page pairs a chart with tabs for the takeaway, the estimates, and the group summaries, and a Data page documents every variable along with retrieval and render dates. Colors are safe for color-blind readers (from the Okabe-Ito palette), and every static chart has alt text.
