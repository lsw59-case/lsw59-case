Question. Among U.S. adults aged 45-79, is a higher LDL cholesterol level associated with higher blood pressure, and does that association differ between men and women or across racial and ethnic groups?

Audience. Students, clinicians, and public-health readers who want a quick, data-grounded look at how two major cardiovascular risk factors relate to each other.

How to use it. Start with the headline numbers above. Compare means and Compare proportions hold the formal tests. Sex & ethnicity answers the main question. Data documents every variable.

Data. NHANES 2017-March 2020 pre-pandemic files, retrieved 2026-09-28. Dashboard last rendered 2026-09-28 20:06 EDT.

Analysis B: systolic BP by LDL of 130 mg/dL or higher

Adults with high LDL (130 mg/dL or higher) have nearly the same average systolic blood pressure as adults with lower LDL. On its own, high LDL does not mark someone as having higher blood pressure.

Difference in mean systolic BP (LDL of 130 or higher minus LDL below 130), Welch t: 0.9 (90% CI: -0.7 to 2.5) mm Hg. The interval includes zero.

Group SDs are 19.5 and 19.1 mm Hg with 566 and 1,421 adults, so Welch’s test (no equal-variance assumption) is the primary result. The bootstrap and rank-based rows check that the conclusion does not hinge on normality.

Average systolic blood pressure does not rise steadily from the lowest to the highest LDL group. LDL group explains just 0.3% of the differences in blood pressure between people, so knowing someone’s LDL category tells you very little about their blood pressure.

One-way ANOVA: F = 1.82 on 3 and 1983 df, p = 0.142. LDL category accounts for 0.3% of the variation in systolic BP.

Answer to the main question

Once age is taken into account, higher LDL goes with slightly higher blood pressure: about 0.36 mm Hg per 10 mg/dL of LDL, or roughly 3.6 mm Hg across a 100 mg/dL range. The relationship looks stronger in men than in women (modest evidence, p = 0.064) and is similar across racial and ethnic groups (p = 0.530).

Interact Test: Does the LDL-blood pressure slope differ between groups?

Model 1: one shared LDL slope for everyone (adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity) Model 2: the same model, but the LDL slope is allowed to differ by sex (or by race/ethnicity)

F measures how much better Model 2 fits the data, relative to the random noise in blood pressure between people. An F around 1 means letting the slopes differ improves the fit by about as much as you’d expect from chance alone. A larger F means a bigger improvement than chance would typically produce.

p is the probability of seeing an improvement at least this large if the slopes were truly the same in every group. A small p means that result would be surprising if the slopes were really equal, which is evidence that they differ. It is not the probability that the slopes are equal.

Across all adults, adjusting for age, sex, and race/ethnicity, each 10 mg/dL higher LDL goes with a 0.36 (90% CI: 0.18 to 0.54) mm Hg difference in mean systolic BP. The interval lies entirely above zero.

Does the slope differ by sex? Interaction test: F = 3.44, p = 0.064.

Does it differ by race/ethnicity? Interaction test: F = 0.79, p = 0.530.

About the data

Source. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, 2017-March 2020 pre-pandemic files: P_DEMO (demographics), P_TRIGLY (LDL; measured only in the morning fasting subsample), and P_BPXO (oscillometric blood pressure). Retrieved with the nhanesA package on 2026-09-28; analysis data prepared 2026-09-28.

Caveats. Survey weights are not used, so results describe this sample, not the U.S. population. Data are cross-sectional, and medication use (statins, blood pressure drugs) is not accounted for.

Accessibility. Color palettes are safe for common color-vision deficiencies, every static chart has alt text, and the layout reflows on phones and tablets.
